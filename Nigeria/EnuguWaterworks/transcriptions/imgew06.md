The Chezy formula is the most reliable one to use generally; the others are inclined to give too high results in the case of small size pipes, although they are the more accurate when considering larger sizes of pipes.

Immediately after the completion of the first installation of the intake works a start was made with laying the supply main, as it was necessary to get water from this source before work could be commenced economically on the construction of the reservoir.

Pipe depots were formed at the reservoir end of the main and at two miles and three miles from the reservoir respectively. From these depots the pipes were carried by head transport and laid alongside the line of trench. The labourers, who were very primitive, found great difficulty in carrying the pipes from the Iva Valley railhead towards the intake, due to the steep inclination of the sides of the valley on this section, and due to their inexperience of working in unison. When carrying first commenced twenty men tried to pack themselves under a pipe without the use of bush sticks: the result can be better imagined than described. The average weight of an eight-inch pipe twelve feet long is 4½ cwts. and gangs of eight men eventually carried one pipe by means of four bush sticks secured at right angles to the pipe.

In one section three-quarters of a mile in length where the pipe line runs alongside the Railway through a shale cutting, it was necessary in the first instance to lay out and examine the pipes on the opposite side of the track to that on which the trench was (being) excavated.

During testing twenty-one cracked pipes were discovered, sixteen of which appeared in this three-quarter mile section.

It is assumed that in several instances the labourers allowed a pipe to drop heavily on the metals when transferring it from one side of the cutting to the other.

The work of transport, excavation, and filling in the trench was let out to contract to the local paramount chief.

GAUGE TANK, RECEIVING TANK AND RESERVOIR.

The supply main empties into a covered gauge tank, six feet by six feet by four feet from which water passes over a “V” notch into a covered receiving tank, twelve feet by twelve feet by seven feet. The receiving tank is connected to the reservoir by a ten-inch diameter pipe ([Figs. 8](imgewfig0608.md) and [9](imgewfig09.md)). The reservoir is 100 feet square, eighteen feet deep with a capacity of 930,000 gallons. Connected to the gauge tank is a still water chamber for the float of a Lea’s Recorder and Integrating Machine capable of registering a flow of 25,000 gallons per hour.
