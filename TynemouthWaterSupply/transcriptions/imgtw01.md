Borough Surveyor’s Office,
Tynemouth,
1st November, 1897.

TYNEMOUTH WATER SUPPLY.

In the year 1786 a number of private individuals, who were styled the Company of
Proprietors of the North Shields Waterworks, obtained an Act of Parliament by
which they were empowered to supply water to the town of North Shields and parts
adjacent. For many years the Company would seem to have fulfilled its obligations
by giving its customers a supply of water of a suitable quality and in sufficient
quantity. In the years 1865–1870, however, owing to circumstances which need not
be here entered into, the Company could not fully meet the demands for water
which were made upon it. In fact so critical was the condition of affairs that
the late Sir John Hawkseley, who had been consulted, reported in January, 1869,
“That the supply was only from 10 to 14 gallons per day per head of population,
and was a condition of things bordering upon a positive want of the first element
of life.”

From that time until the present the water supply of the Borough of Tynemouth has
been an almost constant source of dissatisfaction to all concerned. Time after
time and at the most frequent intervals, complaints were made to the Company by
the Sanitary Authority, as well as by individual customers, of the inadequacy of
the supply, but the Company never boldly faced the difficulties of its position,
but was content to carry out sundry paltry schemes for obtaining water in
comparative driblets from local sources. One by one these sources were, either
voluntarily or under compulsion, abandoned by the Water Company until only two
were left to it, and the large sums of money spent upon these several schemes
amounted to so much money practically wasted.

It is true that under its Act of Parliament the Company was allowed to search for
water within the limits of the Manor of Tynemouth only, but when it was evident
that the sources at its command would no longer yield sufficient water of
suitable quality for the requirements of its district, it was plainly the duty of
the Company to seek to increase its powers to enable it to carry out its
obligations. This, however, the Company not only did not do, but it would appear,
that, without protest, it allowed another Company to appropriate the sources upon
which it had sound, reasonable and just claims.

Situated as it is upon the north bank of the Tyne, it must be evident that in the
first instance Tynemouth had an equal right, with other districts upon the
watershed, to a supply of water from the head waters of the Tyne.
