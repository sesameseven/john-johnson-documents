"We would strongly call attention to the remarkable tendency that towns in the
manufacturing districts have to arrange themselves in groups. Take for example
the group ............ on the Tyne.

This tendency ought always to be considered as an essential element in any
arrangement for a water supply. Such a group of towns, uniting together, may go
for a much better and more perfect scheme than any one of them separately, and
the Legislature ought not only to encourage but as far as possible to compel
such a combination ............ We therefore consider that when any town
belonging to a large group, particularly if forming the nucleus of it, comes to
Parliament for a water bill, the scheme should be so designed as to include the
whole group."

What are the facts in this case? In 1870, that is in the year following that in
which the Royal Commission Report was published, the Newcastle and Gateshead
Water Company promoted a Bill in Parliament, by which it was sought to extend
the gathering ground of the Company so as to include a portion of the watershed
of the North Tyne, which is one of the head waters of the Tyne. In the same Bill
the Newcastle Company, which was already supplying water to the whole of the
districts on the north bank of the Tyne between Newcastle and Wallsend, sought
powers to include the township of Howdon in its area of supply, and was thus
proposing, for the purposes of water supply, to isolate Tynemouth in the far
south-east corner of the County, quite remote from any water-bearing area.

Surely it was then as much to the interest, as it was the duty, of the North
Shields Company, badly as it wanted water, to seek to impose upon the Newcastle
Company the obligation to supply it from those sources to which it had an equal
right with Newcastle and Gateshead, and to oppose the Newcastle Company's Bill,
with all the forces at its command, for that purpose. That such opposition on
the part of the North Shields Company would have been unsuccessful—in face of
the dictum of the Royal Commission which has been quoted—cannot be believed, but
as the Newcastle and Gateshead Water Company's Act of 1870 simply enables that
Company to supply the North Shields Company with water in bulk, subject to a
certain restrictive condition which is set forth, and upon terms to be agreed
upon between the Companies (thus giving the Newcastle Company the power to take
advantage of any straits for water in which the smaller Company might find
itself), it can only be concluded, at this day, that the interests of the Borough
of Tynemouth were then sacrificed by the supineness of the Directorate which at
that time managed the affairs of the North Shields Water Company.

The failure on the part of the North Shields Water Company to obtain a permanent
supply of water from those sources which have been appropriated by the Newcastle
Company, marks the real beginning of the serious difficulties respecting the
water supply with which the ratepayers of the Borough of Tynemouth are now face
to face.

It is quite unnecessary here to inquire closely into the matters which transpired
between the years 1870–1896. It is sufficient to say, in brief, that the record
reveals a long series of complaints of the water supply, and a number of rejected
or abandoned schemes for its improvement. The North Shields Company's own sources
of supply became more and more inadequate, and in 1882 and 1886 agreements
between the local Company and the Newcastle Company were entered into by which
the former was to receive 440,000 gallons daily from the latter. Even with this
additional supply, however, the quantity at the disposal of the North Shields
Company still fell far short of the reasonable requirements of its district, and
pressure was brought to bear upon the Company to induce it to purchase still more
water from the Newcastle Company.
