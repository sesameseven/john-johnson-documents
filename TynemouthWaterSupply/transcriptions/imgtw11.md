But while rainfall caused a decrease of mineral matter in solution, it caused an increase of all the organic impurities, such as ammonia, albuminoid ammonia, and nitrogen, and in the amount of oxygen absorbed: the water became of a marked brownish colour in the worst sample, and its action on lead was rather vigorous. The total amount of organic impurity is large, but considering that its origin is the peaty gathering ground, and that the water is very little exposed to pollution by excrement or other animal refuse, not enough to condemn it. The colour is, however, objectionable, and probably would be objected to by consumers, unless it could be removed, and this, I understand, can be largely if not entirely effected. It cannot be regarded as a high class water, such as Tosson, but it is certainly passable, and would be much improved by settling and filtration. It is very similar in its sources and character to moorland waters, which are supplied to large populations in the West Riding, Lancashire, and in other places nearer home, without ill effects, except that due to its action on lead—a matter to which I shall refer again.

It has already been stated that the water sampled contained most organic matter directly after wet weather. When the most objectionable of these samples was taken, there was a good deal of water in the stream, but it was certainly not in flood. Judging from the evidence already obtained, it seems probable that flood water would contain still larger amounts of organic impurities, and would act more vigorously on lead; but, I understand, arrangements can and will be made by which the first and most objectionable part of the flood water will be sent down the stream and not into the reservoir.

ACTION ON LEAD.—More or less, all waters act upon, or dissolve lead, but the question here is does this water dissolve enough to be a danger to the consumer?

In the West Riding of Yorkshire, and in adjoining counties, moorland peaty waters are extensively used for town supplies, and in some of these districts the lead solvent action of the waters has become of practical importance owing to the occurrence of cases of lead poisoning among the consumers. I therefore requested Messrs. Pattinson to test the lead solvent powers of the samples sent to them, and the results of their experiments are embodied in their reports and letters. One specimen of the Font water, dissolved as much as one-tenth of a grain per gallon, and this is a considerable quantity; but it is to be remembered that the quantity was estimated after the water had stood in a lead pipe for 12 hours, a condition only likely to occur in practice where houses have been unoccupied, when the first water is drawn from the taps in the morning, or where lead cisterns are in use. Moreover, it is only about one-fifth or one-sixth of the lead observed in the water of some of the towns referred to where lead poisoning has occurred, and not more than still occurs in some waters which have been artificially treated to prevent action on lead.

A mass of information has been collected by the Local Government Board as to lead poisoning in the districts mentioned, and containing a population of more than 5,300,000, to whom moorland water was supplied. Filtered water was supplied to 1,900,000 of these, and lead poisoning was noted in districts containing only 34,000 persons. Unfiltered water was supplied to populations amounting to 3,400,000, and lead poisoning was reported in districts containing 809,000 people. It would therefore appear that it occurred almost wholly in districts using unfiltered moorland water, and from another point of view the importance of filtering moorland river water becomes evident.

One or two points in Messrs. Pattinson’s reports remain to be noticed. They compare the action of these waters with that of Newcastle water on lead, and the latter was drawn from the mains in their laboratory. The moorland water of the Newcastle Company is usually, if not always, mixed with water from non-peaty sources, and is filtered before distribution.
