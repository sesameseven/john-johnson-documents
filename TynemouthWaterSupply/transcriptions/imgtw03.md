This the Company did, and the supply from the Newcastle Company’s sources was
increased by from 200,000 to 300,000 gallons per day beyond the quantity
stipulated in the agreement. Nevertheless the district of Tynemouth was still
insufficiently supplied. Complaints were as numerous as ever, and in August,
1896, the Chief Constable reported that at the large fire which had occurred in
Prudhoe Street, there had been a serious lack of water in the Company’s mains,
and that the damage to property would not have been nearly so extensive had
there been water at hand. The Town Council then determined that decisive
measures should be adopted to put an end to such an intolerable state of
affairs.

As a first step in that direction it was resolved to promote a Bill in
Parliament to enable the Corporation to acquire the undertaking of the North
Shields Water Company. It might be here mentioned that the Corporation was
powerless to bring in an improved supply of water so long as the Water Company
enjoyed the right to supply the Tynemouth District. Hence the necessity for
first getting rid of the Water Company. The Ratepayers confirmed the resolution
of the Council, and the Bill was proceeded with.

In March, 1897, it came on for hearing before a Committee of the House of Lords,
when there was every appearance that the proposals of the Tynemouth Corporation
were to be stoutly opposed. The case for the Promoters, however, was so strongly
put, that Counsel for the Opponents of the Bill called no witnesses, but
contented themselves by addressing the Committee.

In stating the case for the Bill, it was, of course, necessary to show why the
compulsory purchase of the undertaking was required, and the Committee was fully
informed of the Company’s position and the poverty of its resources. The
weakness of the Corporation’s case lay in the fact that the Bill included no
provision for improving the water supply, and upon this point Counsel for the
Opponents were not slow to seize. They emphasised it again and again and yet
again, with such effect, that the Committee allowed the Bill to proceed on the
“understanding that the Corporation come before Parliament next year with a Bill
to provide a better water supply” (the exact words of the Chairman are here
quoted).

Those members of the Corporation, who were present at the hearing of the Bill,
took time to consider the Committee’s decision, and acting, not only upon their
own judgment, but upon the advice of Counsel, the undertaking asked for by the
Committee was given and the Bill was allowed to proceed. Endeavours were made to
induce the Committee to extend the time for the promotion of a new Bill to two
years, but the Committee adhered to its original decision.

The Bill in due time was passed by the House of Commons and received the Royal
Assent.

The ostensible and oft repeated reason (and surely it was the real reason,) for
the Corporation seeking to acquire the Water Company’s undertaking was the
desire to improve the water supply of the Borough. “Well then,” said the House
of Lords, in effect “you can have the Water Company’s undertaking if you will
promise to improve the supply.” What else could the representatives of the
Corporation do but give the promise asked for? If they had not, the impression
left upon the minds, not only of the Lords’ Committee, but of the general public
of the Borough of Tynemouth, would have been that the action of the Corporation
was mala fide, that there was no real intention of materially and permanently
improving the water supply, and that the desire to get possession of the Water
Company’s works arose from simple aggressiveness.

There would appear to be an impression in the minds of many that the pledge
given to the Lords’ Committee forms part of the Act, and that its non-fulfilment
will involve a loss to the Corporation of the powers which the Act would
otherwise confer upon it. That is not so. The Act makes no reference whatever to
the pledge.
