After a most careful consideration of Mr. Mansergh’s report and the approximate
financial results likely to be obtained should the Font scheme be adopted, the
Newcastle Company was asked if it would agree to be put under statutory
obligation to supply the Tynemouth Corporation with water in bulk at the rate of
threepence per thousand gallons. The Company replied that it could not agree to
be put under such an obligation, nor could it give a supply at less than
fourpence-halfpenny per thousand gallons, with an added lump sum per annum,
estimated at £500, as interest upon the outlay to which the Company would be put
in laying additional mains for the purpose of such supply. After much
deliberation upon the Newcastle Company’s proposals and Mr. Mansergh’s report,
the Sub-Committee came to the conclusion that it would be to the best interests
of this Borough if a Bill were promoted in Parliament for powers to obtain a
supply of water from the River Font, and it was accordingly decided to submit a
resolution to the Council to that effect.

Tynemouth, in considering the question of improving its water supply, has
therefore two alternatives before it, the first being that of buying from the
Newcastle Company all the water that is required, and the second, that of
obtaining its own supply direct from Nature’s sources.

Before enquiring into the possible results of adopting the first alternative, it
would be as well if the conditions upon which a supply can be obtained from the
Newcastle Company’s sources were made perfectly clear.

By the Newcastle Company’s Act, 1894, it is provided that when the quantity of
water in store in the Company’s reservoirs is less than eight hundred million
and more than seven hundred million gallons, it shall not supply Tynemouth with
more than six hundred thousand gallons daily, (that is something like nine
hundred thousand gallons less than the present requirements), and when the
quantity in store drops to five hundred million gallons, the supply to Tynemouth
must be cut off altogether. It is true that upon the completion of the works it
now has in hand the Newcastle Company will have storage for five thousand
million gallons, and that it is unlikely that for many years the quantity of
water in store can fall as low as eight hundred million gallons, but it is not
certain that such a contingency will not occur.
