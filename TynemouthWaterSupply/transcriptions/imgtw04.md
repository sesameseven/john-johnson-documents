  Immediately after the Bill passed the House of Lords' Committee the Town Council was
formally made aware of what had transpired in the Lords' Committee Room, and the action of the
members who were there present was confirmed by the Council.

  A small Sub-committee was appointed to consider how best the promise, made to the Lords, could
be redeemed. In the first place the Newcastle Company was approached, but the Directors made it
perfectly clear that they would oppose any action which this Corporation might take to put the Company
under statutory obligation to supply water in bulk from its present sources to the Tynemouth district.
When the Newcastle Company was at Parliament in 1889 and in 1894 the Tynemouth Corporation then
endeavoured to have that obligation imposed, but the time for such an application to be successful had
gone by. Tynemouth had already been shut out from those sources in 1870, and the Newcastle and
Gateshead Corporations, and the Authorities representing the other districts which are supplied by the
Newcastle Company, offered so strong an opposition to Tynemouth's proposal in 1889 and 1894, that
it was not accepted by Parliament. Having, therefore, this in view, the Sub-Committee considered
that it would be utterly futile, with the opposition of the Newcastle Company joined to the already
powerful opposition of the Newcastle and Gateshead Corporations, to again ask Parliament to compel
the Newcastle Company to supply the Tynemouth Corporation with water in bulk.

  Other alternatives had therefore to be considered and the Sub-Committee invited Mr. James
Mansergh, of Westminster, the eminent Waterworks Engineer, to submit a scheme for supplying the
Tynemouth District with water.
  
  After consulting Mr. Mansergh, but before receiving his report, information was communicated
to the Sub-Committee that the Newcastle Company was prepared to carry out a scheme for obtaining
water from the Font, and to put itself under statutory obligation to supply water in bulk from that source
to the Tynemouth Corporation, provided that the Corporation was prepared to pay for the
supply at a rate which would justify the Company in proceeding with the scheme-presumably at a
rate which would yield a profit to the Company.

  Before sending any definite reply to that communication the Sub-Committee decided to await
Mr. Mansergh's report, and then ascertain the rate at which the Corporation could itself supply water to
the district.

  In due course Mr. Mansergh's report came to hand. In it he discussed the suitability of the
River Alwin, the Barrow Burn, the River Usway, and the Tosson Springs, (all tributaries of the
River Coquet) and the River Font (a tributary of the Wansbeck), as sources of supply, but the gist
of the report was to the effect that the scheme for obtaining water from the River Font was the most
practicable and feasible that the Borough of Tynemouth could undertake.

  For the information of those who may be unfamiliar with the country, it may be said that the
Font is formed by the junction of two streams, the Fallowlees Burn and the Newbiggin Burn, which
take their rise on the wide area of high lands to the south of the Coquet, and of which Simonside is
the highest summit. The river passes under the high railway viaduct which lies between Ewesley and
Brinkburn Station on the Rothbury Railway; further down it winds its way through the park in which
Nunnykirk Hall is situated, then flows through the village of Netherwitton, and finally joins the
Wansbeck at Mitford.
