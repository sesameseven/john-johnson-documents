# 1. Borough Surveyor’s Report — Raw OCR (Page 521)

Tynemouth Water Supply  
Borough Surveyor’s Office, Tynemouth  
1st November, 1897  

The water supply to Tynemouth and North Shields was first undertaken in 1786 by private individuals, who formed themselves into a company under the title of “The Company of Proprietors of the North Shields Waterworks.”

Between the years 1865 and 1870 the Company were unable to meet the demands made upon them for water. In 1869 Sir John Hawkseley, in a report to the Company, stated that the supply was only 10 to 14 gallons per day per head of the population, and that the supply was quite inadequate to meet the requirements of the district.

From that time until the present the supply has been a source of dissatisfaction to the inhabitants of the district. The Company have made no attempt to improve their sources of supply, and several schemes which have been brought forward have been abandoned.

Tynemouth is situated on the north bank of the Tyne, and the district is supplied with water from the Font. In 1869 the Royal Commission on Water Supply reported that the Font was not a suitable source of supply for the district.

# 2. Medical Officer’s Report — Raw OCR (Page 522)

Report on the Font Water Supply  
J. Edward Gofton, Medical Officer of Health  

Lead solvent action of peaty waters may be stopped by simple processes. Ten years ago Sheffield suffered considerably from lead poisoning due to lead dissolved in the water. It was a peaty moorland water, distributed unfiltered to consumers. To obviate this danger the water was filtered, and a very small quantity of chalk was added at an infinitesimal cost. These measures were eminently successful, and since they were adopted the water has had no action whatever on lead.

Wakefield, Barnsley, and Keighley have similar supplies and similar experiences. The unfiltered water caused lead poisoning; the water now that is filtered and limed does not do so. Oldham, on the other hand, still distributes the water in its natural state and is still subject to lead poisoning.

With these facts before me, I am of opinion that the Font water, provided it is filtered and limed, is suitable for a town supply. As this opinion appears at variance with that expressed by Messrs. Pattinson about one of the samples, it must be noted that the samples were submitted to them in their natural state, and therefore not in the improved condition in which they would be distributed.

The protective deposit mentioned by Mr. Pattinson as forming in water pipes is undoubted, but some time will elapse before it forms in new ones.

The gathering ground and sources of the Font were described in my report to the Council in Committee, dated 23rd August 1887. The conditions remain unaltered. Apart from the peaty surface, the gathering ground of the Font is a favourable one; the number of inhabitants and cattle is very small. There is therefore little danger of pollution by excrement or other animal refuse, and the small quantity of such refuse will be easily dealt with.

### Summary of Opinions

The Font water is not suitable for a town supply in its natural state.  
Settling, continuous efficient filtration, and liming would greatly improve it.  
These measures would check its lead solvent powers and render it suitable.  
Any remaining discolouration would be objectionable more on sentimental than health grounds.  
Additional inquiries into other towns with similar supplies justified modifications of earlier provisional opinions.  
Subject to proper treatment, the water of the Font is suitable for a town supply.

J. Edward Gofton  
Medical Officer of Health

## 📝 Editorial Note

These two reports, presented together, illustrate the dual concerns of the Borough in 1897:  
- **Quantity** — the supply was insufficient for a growing population.  
- **Quality** — untreated Font water posed a risk of lead contamination.

This combined edition preserves the full text of both reports for reference and research.
